Abstract

I set out to discover the link between nanotechnology, the study of extremely small materials and devices operating on a scale of nanometres, and achieving the 17 Sustainable Development Goals set by the United Nations in 2015 to meet by 2030. I looked over findings of other experiments which were investigating the effect of using nanotechnology compared to a standard process e.g. when treating contaminated water. Most of the experiments I read found that nanotechnology, although a new and somewhat unknown technology, provided an effective and more environmentally-friendly alternative to common processes. Although on a small scale, nanotech was already on its way to achieving certain goals such as providing clean water, better sanitation and reducing starvation in local regions. If used on a wider-scale, scientists believe it has a good chance of allowing us to achieve the majority (11 out of 17) of the SDGs. However, it is unlikely to realistically make a big change before 2030, as nanotech is a newer technology and some scientists and citizens are skeptical of side effects it may have for many if used on the global population without rigorous testing. This testing is likely to extend beyond the deadline for the SDGs. Despite this, it can be said that nanotech had a minor role in contributing towards the SDGs in some very local areas where the chosen studies took place.

Main body of the paper

Nanotech's effect on waste management

Nanotechnology has emerged as an effective and sustainable solution for green waste management (turning everyday organic items into healthy products for the environment e.g. composting rotting food. Definition from:A Practical Guide To Green Waste Management Solutions). The need for sustainable waste management is rising as the global population increases (maybe put a graph representing the rising population) and urbanisation (increasing size of cities) spreads. Municipal (city/town with a local government) solid waste (MSW) totalled 2.1 billion metric tons in 2023 and this number is projected to increase by 1.7 billion tonnes by 2050. Use of nanocomposites and nanoparticles allowed for targeted, sustainable removal of pollutants. In Dinesh et al.'s 2026 study, advances in Nano Engineering were shown to be capable of scalable ecosystem restoration and is a promising new technology. The effects of using nanotech adresses several of the UN’s SDGs: 3 (Good Health & Well-being), 6 (Clean Water & Sanitation), 7 (Affordable & Clean Energy), 9 (Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure) & 13 (climate action).

Nanotech's effect on agriculture

Pest and diseases are a considerable threat to global food security as they impact plant health. Damage from pests/diseases directly affects how the plant takes in nutrient, as the plant may have trouble photosynthesising and respiring normally. Normally, the plant uses these nutrients to repair its cells and produce edible products which are sold/eaten. Plant disease and pest activity have become more common and extreme due to global warming. This coupled with changing climates across the world means that some areas are facing very poor growing conditions and the plants can no longer cope. This has serious effect on the economies in affected regions (namely Sub-Saharan Africa: Read this site for further information: Nations Most at Risk from Climate-Induced Food Shortages | Climate Cosmos) and can lead to a lack of food in that local area, nation and perhaps the world. In Khan et al.'s 2026 study, nanomaterials were used to manage some plant diseases as well as detecting plant pathogens. It was found that nanotech provides an effective approach to detecting soil-borne plant diseases.

Use of nanotech has also opened new opportunities for precision-based agriculture (using tech to optimise crop productions & resource management). Saranyadevi et al's 2025 study highlights how nanomaterials & nano-enabled tools transform plant genome (the entire genetic material of an organism: from BBC Bitesize - What is the genome and what does it do?) editing & environmental genomics (“a subfield of genomics that studies the genetic makeup and functional responses of microorganisms in their environment”). Scientists are hopeful that the use of nanomaterials will become more sustainable and safer through future innovation. Management of plant diseases and encouragement of crop protection contributes to better crop yields so may improve food security, health and local ecosystems. This contributes towards SDG 2 (zero hunger), 3 (Good Health and Well Being) & 15 (Life on Land).

Nanotech's effect on contaminated water remediation

The presence of heavy metals in water systems is an increasing and serious global threat to both the environment and public health. Hama et al.'s 2026 study discusses the combination of nanotech and bioremediation technologies (?) to combat and reverse this issue. The natural properties of bacteria, fungi & algae allow them to pull out & detoxify metal pollutants in water. Engineered nanomaterials were used, ones that increase microbial viability and metal uptake and bio-nano hybrids which have an exceptionally strong synergism. It was found that the use of this system effectively remediated contaminants to nearly the same extent as conventional systems. The combination of microorganisms & nanoscience provides an environmentally responsible way to remediate contaminated water. This directly contributes towards SDG 6 (clean water & sanitation), 10 (reducing inequality within countries as poorer people have better access to sanitation, clean drinking water etc), 11 (sustainable cities and communities as water treatment is more env. Sustainable so less waste), 12 (responsible consumption & production, as contaminated water is responsibly treated) & 14 (life below water, less pollutants reach rivers/oceans which protects aquatic life).

Conclusion of the paper

As explored through the multiple studies, nanotechnology has presented itself as a promising, sustainable alternative to many systems currently put in place for processes such as waste management, unsafe water treatment and rearing healthy crops. In its relative infancy it has been proven to make an impact on these mentioned areas and therefore contribute to reaching many of the UN's SDG goals. However, since it is a new technology it requires much more testing to be deemed safe enough to be implemented in full force across the globe. Its effects on peoples' health has not been tracked long term, so it could have negative side effects for many. Developments in the Nanotech field will likely take over 4 years, going past 2030 and therefore past the period when the SDGS were to be met by. Therefore it is unlikely that nanotechnology will contribute heavily towards meeting the SDGS before 2030, as many scientists are skeptical of using it on the masses. But it can be said that it had a minor role in contributing towards the UN's SDGS.
